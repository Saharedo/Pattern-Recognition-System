Day 4: Finding groups with clustering

K-means, hierarchical clustering and DBSCAN, how to check that clusters are valid, and how to turn them into business segments. Includes hand calculations you can repeat in the exam.

1. What clustering is

Clustering is unsupervised: there are no labels. The algorithm groups rows so that rows inside a cluster are similar and rows in different clusters are dissimilar. Goal in one line: minimise intra-cluster distance, maximise inter-cluster distance.

ClassificationClustering
Labels given?Yes, the categories are known up front (for example fraud / not fraud)No, the categories are discovered
GoalLearn a boundary between known classesFind natural structure from similarity
ResultPredicted classGroups you must then name and interpret

Business uses (from the notes): customer segmentation (targeted marketing), market research (lifestyle segments), fraud detection (outliers flagged for review), recommendation (group similar users or products).

Running example, WHO: 24 countries described by literacy, baby mortality, birth rate and death rate. Cluster them into groups (advanced, moderate, borderline, poor) so aid funding can be set per group.

2. Similarity: how "close" is measured

Everything in clustering depends on a distance. Small distance means similar.

Euclidean distance between rows i and j (default for K-means): d(i, j) = sqrt( (x1i − x1j)² + (x2i − x2j)² + ... + (xpi − xpj)² )

Worked example (WHO data)

Australia to Italy: (100−97)² + (7.3−7.6)² + (15−11)² + (8−10)² = 9 + 0.09 + 16 + 4 = 29.09, so the distance is about 5.39. Australia to Somalia: 76² + 118.7² + 31² + 5² = 5776 + 14090 + 961 + 25 = 20,852, so about 144.4. Australia and Italy are clearly alike, Somalia is far away.

No-calculator trickTo compare distances, compare the sums of squares and skip the square root. 29 is far smaller than 20,852, so Italy is the closer country, with no need to compute either root.
Scaling mattersA feature with big numbers (baby mortality up to 126) swamps one with small numbers (death rate around 10). Standardise features before clustering (z-score or min-max). The notes show the closest pair changes after standardising.

Categorical data

  • Ordinal (Poor, Fair, Good, Excellent): map to 1, 2, 3, 4 and use the absolute difference (Manhattan). Poor to Excellent = |1 − 4| = 3.
  • Nominal (lawyer, doctor, teacher): Hamming, match = 0, mismatch = 1.

A centroid is the cluster's centre. It can be the mean (K-means), the median (K-medians), the most central real point (medoid, K-medoids) or the mode (K-modes for categorical data).

3.1 K-means

You choose k (the number of clusters) in advance. Then:

  1. Pick k starting centres (seeds).
  2. Assign every point to its nearest centre.
  3. Update each centre to the mean of the points assigned to it.
  4. Repeat 2 and 3 until nothing changes, the centres barely move, or a maximum number of iterations is reached.

Worked example by hand (one dimension, k = 2)

Points: 1, 2, 9, 10, 12. Start with centres 1 and 10.

  • Assign: 1 and 2 are nearer to 1; 9, 10 and 12 are nearer to 10. Clusters {1, 2} and {9, 10, 12}.
  • Update: new centres = means = 1.5 and (9 + 10 + 12) / 3 = 10.33.
  • Re-assign: nothing changes (9 is 1.33 from 10.33 and 7.5 from 1.5), so the algorithm has converged.
  • SSE (sum of squared distances to centres) = (1−1.5)² + (2−1.5)² + (9−10.33)² + (10−10.33)² + (12−10.33)² = 0.25 + 0.25 + 1.78 + 0.11 + 2.78 = 5.17.

Objective: K-means minimises the within-cluster sum of squared error (SSE, also called WCSS). The SSE never gets worse from one iteration to the next, so the algorithm always converges, but possibly to a local minimum.

WeaknessesYou must choose k. Result depends on the starting centres. It is sensitive to outliers (a far point drags a mean). It prefers round, similar-sized clusters. It needs numeric data.
  • Fix for bad seeds: run several random restarts and keep the lowest SSE, or use K-means++, which spreads the seeds apart (each new seed is picked with probability proportional to its squared distance from the nearest existing seed).
  • Variants: K-medoids (centre is a real point, robust to outliers), K-medians (median per feature, Manhattan distance), K-modes (categorical data, counts mismatches), K-prototypes (mixed numeric and categorical).

3.2 Hierarchical (agglomerative) clustering

  1. Start with every point as its own cluster.
  2. Compute distances between all pairs of clusters.
  3. Merge the two closest clusters.
  4. Repeat until one cluster is left. Record the order in a dendrogram.

Distance between clusters needs a rule, the linkage:

LinkageDistance between clustersSensitivity to outliersTypical shape
Singleclosest pair of pointsHigh (chaining)Long, stringy
Completefarthest pair of pointsHighTight, compact
Averagemean of all pairwise distancesModerateBalanced
Centroiddistance between the centresModerate-
Wardincrease in SSE if mergedLowUniform, round

Pros: no need to fix k in advance, the dendrogram shows structure. Cons: a merge can never be undone, outliers distort it, deciding where to cut is subjective, and it does not scale to large or very high-dimensional data.

3.3 DBSCAN (density-based)

No centroids and no k. Clusters are dense regions; points in sparse regions are labelled noise. Two settings:

  • Eps (ε): the neighbourhood radius.
  • MinPts: the minimum number of points within ε to count as dense.
  1. Pick an unvisited point. If it has at least MinPts neighbours within ε, it is a core point and starts a cluster.
  2. Expand: add every point reachable from it (other core points keep growing the cluster, border points join but do not grow it).
  3. A point that is not reachable from any core point is noise.

Choosing the parameters: MinPts is at least the number of dimensions (2D default 4; more than 2D use 2 × dimensions; more for bigger or noisier data). For ε, plot each point's distance to its k-th nearest neighbour (k = MinPts), sort ascending, and take the elbow as ε.

4. Are the clusters any good? (validation)

Clustering always returns an answer, so you must check it. Two lenses, used together: technical and business.

Technical measures

MeasureWhat it checksGood value
SilhouetteFor each point: a = how close it is to its own cluster, b = how close it is to the nearest other cluster. s = (b − a) / max(a, b).−1 (bad) to +1 (good). Near 0 = between clusters. Above 0.3 is usually acceptable.
Davies-Bouldin (DBI)Average of each cluster's worst-case ratio of spread to separation from its most similar clusterLower is better
Calinski-Harabasz (CH)Between-cluster spread over within-cluster spread (variance ratio)Higher is better
WCSS elbowTotal within-cluster SSE for k = 2, 3, 4...Pick k at the elbow where improvement flattens
Box plots per clusterDo clusters differ clearly on most variables?Distributions should separate
Cluster sizeOne giant cluster may need splitting. Tiny clusters may be outliers.Judgement

Silhouette by hand (using the K-means example above)

Take point 2 in cluster {1, 2}. a = average distance to the other point in its cluster = |2 − 1| = 1. b = average distance to the nearest other cluster {9, 10, 12} = (7 + 8 + 10) / 3 = 8.33. s = (8.33 − 1) / 8.33 = 0.88, a very well-placed point. (The notes also describe a(i) and b(i) using centroid distances. Either way the idea is the same.)

Business lens: profiling the clusters

Numbers are not enough. A cluster is only useful if you can explain it, name it and act on it. Criteria from the notes: distinguishing characteristics, explainable, intuitive label, leads to an action, potential business value, ease of use (fewer clusters are easier).

Exam angleFor any clustering scenario answer in four moves: (1) choose a method and justify it (outliers, shape, need for k, size of data), (2) preprocess (scale, handle categorical features), (3) validate technically (silhouette, elbow, stability), (4) profile for the business (name clusters, tie to actions). For anomaly or fraud detection, DBSCAN's noise points are the anomalies.

5. Practice questions (exam style)

Question A: one K-means round

Six customers have monthly spend (in hundreds): 1, 2, 3, 11, 12, 13. K-means with k = 2 starts with centres 2 and 11. (a) Show the first assignment and the new centres. (b) Has it converged? (c) One new customer spends 100. What does that do to K-means, and what would you change? (4 marks)

Show the worked answer

(a) Distances to centre 2 vs 11: 1 is nearer 2, 2 is at the centre of the first group, 3 is nearer 2 (1 vs 8), 11 is at the second centre, 12 and 13 are nearer 11. Clusters {1, 2, 3} and {11, 12, 13}. New centres: (1 + 2 + 3) / 3 = 2 and (11 + 12 + 13) / 3 = 12.

(b) Re-assigning with centres 2 and 12 gives the same clusters, so yes, it has converged.

(c) The outlier would be assigned to the nearer cluster and drag that centre far to the right (for example from 12 to (11 + 12 + 13 + 100) / 4 = 34), spoiling the cluster. Fix it by removing or capping outliers, using K-medoids or K-medians, or using DBSCAN, which labels it as noise.

Question B: choose and validate

A bank wants to detect unusual card transactions with no labelled fraud cases. (a) Suggest a clustering method and say why. (b) Name two ways to judge whether the result is sensible. (3 marks)

Show the worked answer

(a) DBSCAN. It needs no k, finds dense groups of normal behaviour of any shape, and labels transactions that sit in no dense region as noise, which are the suspicious ones. Scale features first (amount, frequency, location, device).

(b) Technical: a silhouette check (or k-distance plot for choosing ε) and stability across a split-half. Business: review a sample of the flagged transactions with fraud analysts and measure how many are truly suspicious (precision), plus how many known frauds appear (recall) once labels are available.

Quick recall questions
  1. Which clustering method gives noise points and needs no k?
  2. Does a lower or higher Davies-Bouldin value mean better clusters? And Calinski-Harabasz?
  3. Why standardise before clustering?
  4. What does a silhouette near 0 mean? Near −1?
  5. Name one fix for K-means' dependence on starting centres.

Answers: (1) DBSCAN. (2) DBI lower is better, CH higher is better. (3) Large-valued features would dominate the distance. (4) Near 0: the point lies between two clusters. Near −1: probably in the wrong cluster. (5) Several random restarts keeping the lowest SSE, or K-means++.
