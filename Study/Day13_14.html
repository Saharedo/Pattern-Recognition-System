1. Representing a signal with a basis (Day 13)

A signal is a vector of numbers, written in terms of basis vectors. Change the basis and the same signal gets different numbers (coefficients), some of which may be more useful. The idea behind Fourier, wavelets and PCA is the same: choose a good basis.

Decompose: wi = ⟨x, ui⟩ = xᵀ ui = Σj x(j)·ui(j) (dot product with each basis vector) Reconstruct: x̂ = Σ wi · ui Example: x = (1, 2), u1 = (1/√2, 1/√2), u2 = (−1/√2, 1/√2) w1 = (1 + 2)/√2 = 3/√2, w2 = (−1 + 2)/√2 = 1/√2 x̂ = (3/√2)·u1 + (1/√2)·u2 = (1.5 − 0.5, 1.5 + 0.5) = (1, 2) ✓

Energy compaction: a good basis puts most of the signal's energy into a few coefficients, so you can keep the big ones and drop the rest with small error. Learning the basis from data (find the direction u that minimises the total projection error) leads to the eigenvector with the largest eigenvalue of the data's covariance (PCA). Then subtract that component, treat the residual as the new signal and repeat to get the second basis vector, and so on. A learned basis can be viewed as a linear neural network whose weights are the basis vectors.

2. Representation learning

Instead of hand-designing features, learn a mapping from raw data to a useful abstract representation. Good representations are smooth (close inputs map to close outputs), have a suitable dimension (lower to compress, sometimes higher to separate), and become more abstract in deeper layers (raw input → low-level features → high-level concepts → predicted target).

ApproachIdeaWhen
Supervised (human labels)Train on labelled data. To reuse for a new task: linear adaptation (freeze the pretrained network f, train only a new linear layer on the new data) or fine-tuning (start from f and continue training on the new data).Plenty of labels. Small target data: use the pretrained network.
Self-supervisedThe data itself provides the training signal through a pretext task (reconstruct the input, match augmented views). The learned features are then used for the target (downstream) task, which usually needs a few labels.Lots of unlabelled data, few labels.

Two self-supervised styles: generative (encoder compresses, decoder reconstructs the input, minimise reconstruction error: the autoencoder) and contrastive (pull together two augmented views of the same item, push apart different items).

4. Anomaly detection

An anomaly (outlier) is an observation so different from the rest that it looks as if it was produced by a different mechanism. Typical uses: medical diagnosis, fraud, manufacturing defects, machine health.

Type of anomalyMeaningExample
PointOne value far from all othersA spike of 10x normal vibration
ContextualNormal in general, abnormal in its context30 °C in winter
CollectiveEach point looks fine, but together they are unusualFlat signal for 1 second

Output: a binary label (normal or anomaly) or a score (ranks cases but needs a threshold).

ApproachAssumptionScoreLabels needed
Geometric (clustering)Normal points sit in clusters, anomalies do notDistance from the nearest cluster centreNo
StatisticalNormal data lies in high-probability regions of a fitted modelLow probability, or beyond mean ± 4σ; change in the distribution over a sliding windowNo
ClassificationA classifier can separate normal from abnormalClassifier scoreYes, normal and abnormal
AutoencoderAnomalies are rare, so a model trained on normal data reconstructs them badlyReconstruction errorNo (train on normal data)

Judging a detector: because anomalies are rare, accuracy is useless (always saying "normal" scores 99.9%). Use recall of anomalies, precision, F1, and the ROC or precision-recall curve, and choose the threshold from the cost of a missed anomaly against a false alarm.

Detection vs diagnosisDetection says something is wrong. Diagnosis says what is wrong, which needs labelled causes (sample Q2d uses Bayesian classification on maintenance records).

5. Sample Q2c worked through: conveyor anomaly detection

Data: 3,000 normal sequences and 2 abnormal ones. Each sequence is 4 s at 50 Hz (200 samples). Features: a 10-bin histogram of the signal values plus 20 wavelet statistics (4 statistics × 5 subbands), concatenated to 30 dimensions. The colleague proposes a multilayer perceptron for two-class classification. Do you agree?

Answer: not as proposed. Supervised classification needs enough examples of every class, and here the abnormal class has 2 out of 3,002 (about 1 in 1,500).

Challenge 1: extreme class imbalance and almost no abnormal examples

  • Problem: the network can score 99.93% by always predicting "normal" and never learn what abnormal looks like. Two samples cannot be split into train, validation and test, and cannot represent the many ways a machine may fail (future anomalies may differ from these two).
  • Solutions: switch to anomaly detection trained on normal data only: an autoencoder on the 30-dimensional features (or on the raw signal) with a reconstruction-error threshold, or a one-class method, a Gaussian model or a clustering distance. Use the 2 abnormal sequences only to test the detector and to sanity-check the threshold. If a classifier is still wanted, create more abnormal examples (time-warping, jittering and scaling augmentation, or synthetic anomalies) and use recall, precision and F1, not accuracy.

Challenge 2: the chosen features lose information (justify from Figure 1)

  • Histogram: bin 1 shows a clear difference (abnormal about 63 against normal about 19), which matches the raw signal: the abnormal trace sits flat at about 0 between about 2.0 and 2.7 s. But a histogram ignores time order, so two very different waveforms can give the same histogram, and most other bins overlap heavily.
  • Frequency features: the two curves are almost the same except around features 3 and 4 (about 1.45 and 2.1 for abnormal against about 0.95 and 0.9 for normal), and most of the 20 values are near zero, so the discriminating information is concentrated in very few dimensions. With only two abnormal cases it is impossible to know which differences are reliable.
  • Solutions: keep the temporal structure by using a 1D convolutional or LSTM autoencoder on the raw sequence, add time-frequency features that keep location (wavelet coefficients over time) and a feature for run lengths, and use DTW or other shift-tolerant similarity because normal sequences also differ in phase. Select the features that vary most between normal and abnormal using the available examples and domain knowledge.

Challenge 3 (optional): per-sequence normalisation

The plotted amplitude is normalised to 0 to 1, which removes absolute amplitude. If a failure changes the overall level (for example a loose part vibrating harder), that is hidden. Keep the absolute level as a separate feature.

Exam angle"Do you agree?" needs a clear yes or no, then reasons tied to the numbers (3,000 against 2) and the figure (name a feature and its values). Give each challenge with a matching solution, and mention evaluation (recall, precision, F1, threshold chosen from the cost of a miss against a false alarm).

6. Multimodal sensing and sensor fusion (Day 14)

A modality is a way something is expressed or perceived: audio, image, inertial motion, text, temperature. Raw modalities (closest to the sensor) can be turned into abstract ones (detected objects, sentiment, event statistics).

Single modalityTypical tasks
Inertial (accelerometer, gyroscope)Gesture recognition, activity recognition, screen orientation
AudioSound classification, speaker recognition, keyword detection, command and control, machine anomaly detection (e.g. SMD assembly machine sound at 192 kHz)
VisionScene and object recognition, gesture, anomaly (defects), video summarisation, flood monitoring (210 water-level sensors and 49 CCTV cameras)

Data augmentation for sensor windows: scaling (multiply by a random factor), magnitude filtering (smooth random curve around 1), jittering (add noise to mimic sensor noise), time-warping (smoothly stretch the time axis). They make models more robust to multiplicative noise, additive noise and timing changes.

Why fuse sensors?

  • Representation: the fused information is at a higher level of abstraction.
  • Accuracy: the spread of the fused estimate is usually smaller than from any single source.
  • Completeness: new information gives a more complete view.

Kinds of fusion: across sensors (several temperature sensors on one object), across domains (same attribute, different ranges), across attributes (temperature, pressure and humidity together give air refractive index), across time (current plus historical readings).

When to combine: three architectures

FusionWhere the modalities meetProsCons
Early (tightly coupled)Raw data or features are joined, then one recogniser decidesLearns cross-modal interactions, acts like one complex sensorNeeds synchronised, compatible data; one noisy modality hurts everything
Intermediate / restricted (loosely coupled)One modality's result narrows the other's searchSaves computation, higher accuracy through contextPipeline order and errors propagate
Late (uncoupled)Each modality gets its own full recogniser; decisions are combined at the endSimple, reuses existing pipelines, tolerates a missing modalityCannot exploit low-level cross-modal interactions
EarlyData A + Data B → join features → one model → decision.
RestrictedData A → recogniser A → context → Data B recogniser → decision.
LateData A → model A, Data B → model B → combine outputs → decision.

Multimodal learning challenges

  • Representation: summarise heterogeneous data (each modality differs in noise, structure and format).
  • Alignment: find the connections between elements. Spatial alignment puts positions in a common coordinate system. Temporal alignment puts times on a common axis (use DTW). In feature space, CLIP trains an image encoder and a text encoder to give matching pairs high cosine similarity and mismatched pairs low similarity (400 million image-text pairs).
  • Reasoning, generation, transference: compose knowledge from several modalities, produce one modality from another, transfer knowledge between modalities.
  • Practical problems: synchronisation, missing or noisy data, scale, computational cost.

8. Practice questions (exam style)

Question A: autoencoder threshold

An autoencoder is trained on normal motor current signals. Reconstruction errors on five normal validation signals are 0.02, 0.03, 0.025, 0.04 and 0.03. (a) Choose a threshold using mean + 3 standard deviations. (b) A new signal has error 0.12. What do you decide? (c) Why is the autoencoder trained on normal data only? (4 marks)

Show the worked answer

(a) Mean = (0.02 + 0.03 + 0.025 + 0.04 + 0.03) / 5 = 0.145 / 5 = 0.029. Deviations: −0.009, 0.001, −0.004, 0.011, 0.001. Squares (×10⁻⁶): 81, 1, 16, 121, 1, sum 220 so variance 44 × 10⁻⁶ and standard deviation ≈ 0.0066. Threshold ≈ 0.029 + 3 × 0.0066 ≈ 0.049. (In the exam an estimate such as "about 0.05" with the working shown is fine.)

(b) 0.12 is far above 0.049, so flag it as an anomaly.

(c) Anomalies are rare and varied, so there are not enough labelled examples. Training on normal data teaches the network to reconstruct normal patterns well. Anything unfamiliar reconstructs badly and gives a large error.

Question B: choose a fusion style

A smart factory combines a microphone and a vibration sensor to detect a machine fault. The microphone sometimes fails. (a) Which fusion architecture would you use and why? (b) Name two practical problems of combining the two sensors. (3 marks)

Show the worked answer

(a) Late fusion: each sensor has its own model and the decisions are combined, so the system still works when the microphone fails and each pipeline can be built with existing tools. (Early fusion would be better if the sensors are well synchronised and the interaction between them is informative.)

(b) Synchronisation and alignment (different sampling rates such as 192 kHz audio against 50 Hz vibration, handled by resampling or DTW), and different noise levels or missing data from one sensor.

Quick recall questions
  1. What is the difference between linear adaptation and fine-tuning?
  2. What score does an autoencoder anomaly detector use?
  3. Name the three anomaly types.
  4. Which fusion type joins data at the very last stage?
  5. Why is accuracy a poor metric for anomaly detection?

Answers: (1) Linear adaptation freezes the pretrained network and trains only a new linear layer; fine-tuning continues training the whole network. (2) Reconstruction error. (3) Point, contextual, collective. (4) Late (uncoupled) fusion. (5) Anomalies are so rare that predicting "normal" always gives very high accuracy.
