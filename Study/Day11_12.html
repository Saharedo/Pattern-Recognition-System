Days 11 and 12: Signals, transforms and statistical sensing

Sampling, peaks, dynamic time warping, Fourier and wavelet features, and Bayesian classification of sensor data. These are the building blocks behind sample questions 2c and 2d.

1. Signals and sensors

A signal is a quantity that varies over time or space and carries information: heart electrical activity (ECG), vibration, temperature, sound, a camera image. A sensor turns a physical quantity into a signal. To a computer a signal is just numbers. Sense making means extracting meaning from them and then acting: predict, take action, or suggest actions.

Signals are continuous in the real world but are sampled at set time steps and quantised into digital values (discrete-time, discrete-value). A signal is periodic if f(t + T) = f(t) for all t, even if f(t) = f(−t) (symmetric), odd if f(t) = −f(−t). Superposition and decomposition: complicated signals can be built by scaling and adding simpler ones, and studied by splitting them into simple parts.

2. Sampling and the Nyquist rule

Nyquist-ShannonTo capture a signal whose highest frequency is fmax, sample at at least 2 × fmax. Below that, high frequencies are misrepresented as lower ones (aliasing).

Worked examples:

  • Telephone speech with a maximum frequency of 4 kHz must be sampled at 2 × 4 = 8 kHz.
  • A signal with bandwidth 10 kHz needs a rate of at least 20 kHz.
  • Sample paper Q2c link: vibration sampled at 50 Hz can represent frequencies only up to 25 Hz (50 / 2). A 4-second sequence has 4 × 50 = 200 samples.

3. Finding peaks and fixing the baseline

3.1 Peaks and troughs

Peaks are significant events (a heartbeat, a demand spike, a load surge). Troughs are inverted peaks. For a continuous signal a peak is a maximum: the slope f′(x) = 0 and changes from + to −. A discrete signal has no derivative, so use finite differences:

forward difference f(x+Δx) − f(x) backward f(x) − f(x−Δx) central [f(x+Δx) − f(x−Δx)] Signal [1, 2, 5, 7, 4, 2] → differences [1, 3, 2, −3, −2] Sign goes + (+2) to − (−3) between 5, 7, 4: the peak is the value 7.

Finite differences find every small wiggle, including noise. So in practice use a peak finder (scipy.signal.find_peaks) with filters:

FilterKeeps only peaks that...Weakness
heightare above a levelSignal level varies, so peaks of normal events can be missed
distanceare at least d samples apartDepends on the use case, might pick a smaller peak
prominencestand out enough from the surrounding signalNeeds a sensible threshold; usually the best option
widthare wide enough (measured at half the prominence)Rejects sharp spikes

Prominence = how far a peak rises above the higher of its two bases. Find the peak's two bases by extending a horizontal line left and right until it meets a higher peak (or the edge) and taking the lowest point on each side. The higher of the two bases is the "lowest contour line", and prominence is peak height minus that.

3.2 Baseline correction

Sensors drift (temperature, humidity, cheap hardware), so the baseline wanders and spoils peak detection and comparisons. Correct it by estimating the baseline z and subtracting: ycorrected = y − z.

  • Asymmetric least squares (ALS) finds a smooth z close to y by minimising S = Σ wi(yi − zi)² + λ Σ (Δ²zi)². The first term is fit to the data, the second penalises roughness, and λ sets the balance.
  • The weights are asymmetric: points above the fitted baseline (peaks) get a small weight p and points below get 1 − p, so peaks do not drag the baseline up. Suggested values: 0.001 ≤ p ≤ 0.1, 10² ≤ λ ≤ 10⁹, 5 to 10 iterations.

4. Comparing signals: Dynamic Time Warping (DTW)

Euclidean distance compares point i of one signal with point i of the other. It needs equal lengths, and two identical shapes slightly out of step look very different. DTW stretches or squeezes the time axis to find the best alignment, so similar shapes match even when they are shifted or at different speeds. It is used in speech and signature recognition.

The algorithm

  1. Distance matrix: D(i, j) = (yi − xj)² for every pair.
  2. Accumulated cost: A(i, j) = D(i, j) + min( A(i−1, j), A(i, j−1), A(i−1, j−1) ). First row and column just add up along the edge.
  3. Warping path: trace back from the end to the start. Rules: it starts at (1, 1) and ends at (n, m), it never goes backwards (monotonic), and it has no gaps (continuous steps). The DTW distance is the final A(n, m).

Worked example

x = [0, 2, 3] (columns), y = [0, 0, 2, 3] (rows). Different lengths, so Euclidean distance is impossible. Same shape, though: y just holds its first value one step longer.

Three ways to represent a signal: (1) hand-designed transforms (today: Fourier, wavelets), (2) adaptive to the input, (3) learned from data (PCA-like bases, autoencoders, next guide).

5.1 Fourier transform: what frequencies are present?

Any signal can be split into sinusoids of different frequencies. The Fourier transform gives, for each frequency, how much of it is in the signal. It moves the view from the time domain to the frequency domain.

DFT: F(u) = Σ f(x) · e^(−i2πux/N), u = 0 ... N−1 e^(ix) = cos x + i sin x Worked example: f = [2, 3, 4, 4] F(0) = 2 + 3 + 4 + 4 = 13 (the sum, i.e. the average level times N) F(1) = 2 + 3e^(−iπ/2) + 4e^(−iπ) + 4e^(−i3π/2) = 2 − 3i − 4 + 4i = −2 + i F(2) = −1 F(3) = −2 − i Magnitude |F(1)| = sqrt((−2)² + 1²) = sqrt(5) ≈ 2.24
LimitationFourier tells you which frequencies exist but not when. A signal with 5 Hz then 25 Hz one after the other has the same frequency content as one with both at the same time. For signals whose frequency changes (vibration, speech, ECG), use a time-frequency method such as the wavelet transform.

5.2 Wavelet transform: which frequencies, and when?

A wavelet is a short wave that goes above and below the axis and has limited duration (unlike a sinusoid, which lasts forever). The signal is passed through two filters, then down-sampled by 2:

  • Low-pass filter (smoothing, averaging) gives approximation coefficients A.
  • High-pass filter (differencing) gives detail coefficients D.
  • Repeat on the approximation to get several levels (subbands). Reconstruction reverses the process.

Worked example: Haar wavelet on [9, 7, 3, 5]

Haar filters: low-pass [1/√2, 1/√2], high-pass [−1/√2, 1/√2] (the notes' convention, which yields second minus first).

LevelPairsApproximation (sum / √2)Detail ((second − first) / √2)
1(9, 7)16/√2 = 8√2(7 − 9)/√2 = −√2
1(3, 5)8/√2 = 4√2(5 − 3)/√2 = √2
2(8√2, 4√2)12√2/√2 = 12(4√2 − 8√2)/√2 = −4

Wavelet coefficients: [12, −4, −√2, √2] (final approximation, level-2 detail, then the level-1 details). Check: signal energy 81 + 49 + 9 + 25 = 164 and coefficient energy 144 + 16 + 2 + 2 = 164, so energy is preserved. Most of it sits in the first coefficient: energy compaction.

Uses of wavelets:

  • Feature extraction: decompose, then take statistics of each subband (mean, median, variance, standard deviation) as features. 5 subbands × 4 statistics = 20 features, exactly the frequency-domain feature in sample Q2c.
  • Denoising: shrink small detail coefficients (noise) and reconstruct. Augmentation in the wavelet domain. Fixed wavelet filters inside CNNs.

6. Statistical classification of sensor data (sample Q2d)

Given a measurement x and classes ω1, ω2, decide which class is more probable using Bayes' rule:

P(ω | x) = P(x | ω) · P(ω) / P(x) posterior = likelihood × prior / evidence Decision: choose the class with the larger P(ω | x). P(x) is the same for all classes, so compare P(x | ω)·P(ω).

With several features, the naive assumption (features independent given the class) lets you multiply likelihoods. For categorical features, estimate each likelihood by counting inside the class. For numeric features, model each class with a Gaussian and learn its mean and variance from the training data:

p(x | class) = 1 / sqrt(2πσ²) · exp( −(x − μ)² / (2σ²) ) μ, σ² estimated per class

7. When one Gaussian is not enough: mixtures and EM

If the data forms several blobs, one Gaussian fits badly. A Gaussian mixture model (GMM) uses several Gaussians, each with a weight. The problem: you do not know which blob each point came from (a hidden label), and you need the labels to fit the Gaussians. Expectation-Maximisation (EM) solves both at once:

  1. Initialise the parameters (guess).
  2. E-step: for every point compute the probability it belongs to each component (a "soft" label), P(component | x) ∝ P(x | component) · P(component).
  3. M-step: re-estimate each component's parameters and weight using those soft labels as weights.
  4. Repeat 2 and 3 until it stops changing (or a set number of iterations).

Dice example from the notes. A caller picks a yellow or blue die at random and announces the roll. Start with guesses P(6 | Yellow) = 0.4, P(6 | Blue) = 0.1 and P(Yellow) = P(Blue) = 0.5. For a roll of 6: Yellow 0.4 × 0.5 = 0.2, Blue 0.1 × 0.5 = 0.05, normalise to P(Yellow | 6) = 0.8 and P(Blue | 6) = 0.2. After doing this for all 18 rolls, the M-step adds up the soft counts (yellow total 7.31 of 18) and sets P(Yellow) = 0.41, P(Blue) = 0.59, and re-computes each die's probabilities (e.g. P(1 | Yellow) = 1.71 / 7.31 = 0.234).

K-means is the "hard label" cousin of EM: each point belongs to exactly one cluster. EM gives soft memberships and fits an elliptical shape per cluster. GMM also helps with anomaly detection (points with very low probability under the fitted mixture).

8. Practice questions (exam style)

Question A: sampling and features

A bearing vibration sensor produces signals with useful content up to 120 Hz. (a) What is the minimum sampling rate? (b) You sample at 100 Hz for 2 seconds. How many samples, and why is this a problem? (c) The signal is not stationary: the frequency changes during the recording. Why is wavelet analysis better than the Fourier transform here? (4 marks)

Show the worked answer

(a) At least 2 × 120 = 240 Hz.

(b) 100 × 2 = 200 samples. A rate of 100 Hz is below 240 Hz, so the highest frequencies are aliased (they appear as false lower frequencies) and the real content above 50 Hz is lost.

(c) The Fourier transform shows which frequencies are present but not when they occur. Wavelets use short, localised waves at several scales, so they show both frequency and time location, which is what a changing signal needs.

Question B: Haar wavelet

Apply a two-level Haar decomposition to [4, 2, 6, 8] using low-pass [1/√2, 1/√2] and high-pass [−1/√2, 1/√2]. Give the coefficients and check the energy. (4 marks)

Show the worked answer

Level 1: (4, 2): approx 6/√2 = 3√2, detail (2 − 4)/√2 = −√2. (6, 8): approx 14/√2 = 7√2, detail (8 − 6)/√2 = √2.

Level 2: approximations (3√2, 7√2): approx 10√2/√2 = 10, detail (7√2 − 3√2)/√2 = 4.

Coefficients: [10, 4, −√2, √2]. Energy check: original 16 + 4 + 36 + 64 = 120, coefficients 100 + 16 + 2 + 2 = 120. Equal.

Question C: Bayes on sensor states

A pump log (Vibration, Temperature, Cause) has 8 records. Fault (3 records): (High, High), (High, Low), (Low, High). Normal (5 records): (Low, Low), (Low, Low), (High, Low), (Low, High), (Low, Low). (a) Using a naive Bayes classifier, decide the state for Vibration = High and Temperature = Low. (b) What would you do if P(Vibration = High | Fault) were 0? (4 marks)

Show the worked answer

(a) Priors: Fault 3/8, Normal 5/8. Fault: P(Vib = High | Fault) = 2/3, P(Temp = Low | Fault) = 1/3. Normal: P(Vib = High | Normal) = 1/5, P(Temp = Low | Normal) = 4/5.

Fault score = 3/8 × 2/3 × 1/3 = 6/72 = 1/12 ≈ 0.083. Normal score = 5/8 × 1/5 × 4/5 = 20/200 = 1/10 = 0.10. Normal is larger, so the decision is Normal. Ratio 1/12 : 1/10 = 10 : 12, so P(Normal | x) is 6/11 ≈ 0.55 and P(Fault | x) is 5/11 ≈ 0.45, a close call.

(b) The zero would force the Fault score to 0 regardless of other evidence. Apply Laplace smoothing: add 1 to each count in the numerator and the number of values (2) to the denominator, so P(Vib = High | Fault) = (0 + 1) / (3 + 2) = 0.2.

Quick recall questions
  1. State the Nyquist sampling rule.
  2. What does the DC (first) Fourier coefficient F(0) equal?
  3. Which Haar filter gives smoothing and which gives edges?
  4. What are the two EM steps?
  5. Which peak filter measures how much a peak stands out from its surroundings?

Answers: (1) Sample at least twice the highest frequency. (2) The sum of the signal values (average level times N). (3) Low-pass smooths (averages), high-pass gives details (differences). (4) Expectation (estimate hidden labels) and Maximisation (update parameters). (5) Prominence.
