Part A. Ensembles and hybrid models (Day 10, sample Q1c)

1. The idea

An ensemble is a committee of models. Each makes a prediction and the group decides: majority vote for classes, average for numbers. A committee is usually more accurate and more reliable than any one member.

Dietterich's conditionAn ensemble beats its members if and only if the members are accurate and diverse (they make different mistakes). Identical models add nothing.

Why voting helps (worked example). Three independent classifiers, each 70% accurate. The majority is right when at least 2 of 3 are right:

P = 3 · 0.7² · 0.3 + 0.7³ = 3 · 0.49 · 0.3 + 0.343 = 0.441 + 0.343 = 0.784

The committee scores 78.4%, better than any single 70% model. The gain shrinks if the models make the same mistakes, which is why diversity matters.

2. Bias and variance: why averaging works

High bias (underfit)High variance (overfit)
Training fitPoorVery good
Sensitivity to the training sampleLow: changing a few points changes littleHigh: the model changes a lot
Typical modelToo simple (shallow tree, line)Too complex (deep unpruned tree)

Expected error = bias² + variance + irreducible noise. Averaging many models reduces variance without raising bias. So you take flexible, low-bias models (deep trees) and average away the variance. The reduction is smaller than 1/N because the models are correlated.

BaggingResample the data N times → train N models side by side → vote.

  • Bagging helps unstable models (deep trees, neural nets). It does little for stable ones (linear models, KNN). Usually under 50 models is enough.
  • Boosting in short: weight all rows equally → train → increase weights on the rows it got wrong → train a new model on the reweighted data → repeat → combine with weights. Gradient boosting (for example XGBoost) instead fits each new model to the remaining error using gradient descent on a loss function.
  • Random forest out-of-bag (OOB) error: each tree leaves out about a third of the rows. Test each tree on its own left-out rows and combine. You get an error estimate with no separate test set.

4. Variable importance in a forest or boosted model

  • Impurity-based: add up how much each variable reduced Gini impurity (or entropy) across all splits and trees. Better than just counting how often it appears.
  • Permutation: shuffle one variable in the out-of-bag data and measure how much accuracy drops. A big drop means the variable matters. Works for any model.

Difference: the tree says buying is about PageValue and BounceRate and ignores ProductRelated entirely, while AdaBoost spreads credit widely and ranks ProductRelated first.

Possible reasons:

  • A single tree is greedy: once PageValue takes the first split, correlated features (ProductRelated and its duration, BounceRate and ExitRate) add little and never get used. Importance is masked.
  • AdaBoost trains many trees on re-weighted data, so later trees must fix the earlier mistakes and use other variables. Its importance is summed over all those trees.
  • Impurity-based importance favours variables with many distinct values or levels (Browser, OperatingSystems look surprisingly important), so part of the score may be a bias, not real signal.
  • The flat scores suggest no variable dominates once all the trees are combined, so the ensemble uses many weak signals.

How to use the information in further modelling:

  • Feature selection: drop the lowest-ranked (Weekend, Region, Month, TrafficType) to get a leaner, faster model and then compare accuracy with cross-validation.
  • Train the SVM, neural network or logistic regression on the top variables to cut noise and overfitting.
  • Cross-check with permutation importance before trusting the ranking, and keep variables that the business knows matter (PageValue).

6. Hybrid models (shorter)

  • Ensemble: many models of the same kind (weak, homogeneous). Hybrid: different kinds of technique combined (heterogeneous).
  • Multiple classifier systems and stacking: several expert models, then a controller or learner combines their outputs. Mixture of experts: a gating network picks or weights specialist sub-networks for each input (used in large language models).
  • Self-tuning: one technique tunes another, for example a genetic algorithm choosing network layers and weights, or a neural net that learns fuzzy rules (neuro-fuzzy).
  • Cooperating experts: the output of one feeds another (a neural net recognises a pile shape that a fuzzy controller uses).
  • Generative + discriminative: discriminative models (logistic regression, SVM, deep classifiers) learn P(y | x) and give strong accuracy but need lots of labels. Generative models (Naive Bayes, Gaussian mixtures, VAEs, GANs) learn P(x, y), cope with small, imbalanced or missing data and can make synthetic samples. Combine them, for example GAN images to boost a rare class for a classifier.

Part B. Deep learning applications and deep reinforcement learning (Day 5, sample Q2b)

7. Three jobs for deep learning

JobWhat it doesExamples
IdentifyClassify, detect (bounding boxes), segmentAmazon Go shops, face recognition, plant disease, traffic offences, document review
CreateGenerative AI makes text, images, audio, videoChatGPT-style models, GauGAN, deepfakes, music and speech generation
ActLearn what to do from feedbackRobot grasping and throwing, game agents, autonomous driving, forecasting that triggers decisions

GAN idea: a generator makes fakes, a discriminator tries to tell fake from real, and each improves by competing. Uses: synthetic training images, but also deepfakes (an ethics risk). A classification model alone gets confused by other objects in the image, so you add localisation (bounding boxes) or segmentation.

8. Reinforcement learning in one picture

  • State: what the agent observes. Action: what it can do. Reward: the score for the result. Policy: the rule it learns for choosing actions.
  • Explore vs exploit: try new actions to discover better ones, or repeat the best known one.
  • Deep Q-learning uses a neural network to estimate the expected reward of each action in each state. Continuous control (joint movements) often uses actor-critic methods such as DDPG.
  • Examples from the notes: FIFA free kicks (deep Q-learning), game agents that taught themselves, robots learning to throw and grasp (and sharing experience across robots to learn faster), a mobile robot learning collision avoidance (with human feedback), humanoids learning football, Wayve's self-driving car (learns from safety-driver interventions).

(ii) Architecture (required parts: physical robot, target object, other objects, identification system, decision-making system, others):

The agent's network is multi-layer, as required: for example a CNN encoder for the image part of the state followed by dense layers producing joint commands (and a critic network in an actor-critic design). The identification classifier is the model from part (a).

(iii) Reducing the danger of real physical training:

  • Train in simulation first (a digital twin of the arm, belt and obstacles). Collisions cost nothing there. Use domain randomisation (vary lighting, textures, object positions) so the policy transfers to the real robot (sim-to-real), then fine-tune on the real arm at low speed.
  • Safety layer and constraints: hard limits on joint angles, speed and force, protected zones around humans, emergency stop. The agent can never send an unsafe command, so it can explore without damage.
  • Penalise proximity, not only impact using the cameras' tracked distances, so it learns to keep clear before any collision happens.
  • Learn from demonstrations or human feedback (imitation learning, DRL with human feedback) to start with a sensible policy and avoid random flailing.
  • Curriculum: start with one slow object and no obstacles, add difficulty gradually. Share experience across several robots or use logged data (offline RL) to cut real-world trials.

Practice questions (exam style)

Question A: bagging or boosting?

A bank has a noisy dataset with many mislabelled records and needs a robust fraud model. (a) Would you pick bagging or boosting, and why? (b) A random forest has 300 trees. Explain how you can estimate its error without a test set. (c) Three independent classifiers have accuracy 80% each. What is the majority-vote accuracy? (5 marks)

Show the worked answer

(a) Bagging or a random forest. Boosting keeps raising the weight of rows it gets wrong, so it chases the mislabelled rows and can overfit the noise. Bagging averages independent models and is robust to noise.

(b) Out-of-bag error: each tree was trained on a bootstrap sample (about two thirds of the data). Each row was left out of about a third of the trees. Predict each row using only the trees that did not see it, take the majority vote, and compare to the true label. The fraction wrong is the OOB error.

(c) P(at least 2 of 3 right) = 3·0.8²·0.2 + 0.8³ = 3·0.64·0.2 + 0.512 = 0.384 + 0.512 = 0.896, about 89.6%.

Question B: reward design

A warehouse robot learns with deep reinforcement learning to carry boxes along a corridor shared with people. (a) Give three reward components with their sign. (b) The robot must not be trained by crashing into people. Give two ways to avoid that. (4 marks)

Show the worked answer

(a) (1) Positive reward for delivering a box to the right place, with a small time penalty each step (efficiency). (2) Large negative for any contact with a person or shelf, and graded negative for coming closer than a safe distance. (3) Small negative for jerky motion, high torque or leaving the battery too low (protects hardware).

(b) Train in a simulated warehouse with simulated people, then transfer with domain randomisation. Add a safety layer with speed limits and automatic stopping near people, so unsafe actions are blocked. (Also acceptable: learning from human demonstrations, or proximity penalties instead of real contact.)
